Olga Korosteleva
— Vice Chair of ASA District 6, Professor at California State University, Long Beach
ASA District 6 Vice Chair's Initiatives

This presentation introduces the current Vice Chair of ASA District 6 (2026-2028 term), and provides an overview of the district’s structure, chapters, and current ASA initiatives. It highlights opportunities such as DataFest, traveling courses, poster competitions, and the visitation program. The presentation also outlines proposed District 6 initiatives designed to connect students and early-career professionals with statisticians across a range of industries. These include a career-focused symposium, a statistics-themed game show, a student mentorship program, a research paper competition, and a research opportunity for high-achieving high school students. The presentation concludes by recognizing the Montana Chapter’s 50-year legacy.

Owen Ward
— Simon Fraser University
Continuous Latent Space Representations of Networks from Event Data

Network data often consists of underlying continuous time interactions between the nodes in the network. Representing the true underlying dynamics of such interactions is challenging, and often instead utilises an aggregate summary of these events. We propose a continuous time functional latent space model for the nodes in a network, based directly on these underlying interactions, using modern computation methods to scale inference to large scale networks commonly seen in practice. We validate our proposed model and interpret the results for real interactions on networks, identifying interpretable latent structure.

Nairanjana Dasgupta
— Washington State University
r-Power an Alternative Way to Look at Multiplicity

In these days of large-scale testing, there is always a question of how we control for Type I errors. Many methods abound in the literature for this issue. In this talk, I will introduce a different way of looking at multiplicity, which relies on reliability of a list rather than significance level. This was introduced by Dasgupta, Lazar and Genz (Sankhya, 2016) and has been followed up by Sijuwade, Chakraborty and Dasgupta (Metrika, 2023). I will do a short presentation of the basic idea of r-power and how we are using it in GenAI.

Erin Lipman
— University of Michigan
Statistics Careers in Government and Health Policy

There are many opportunities for people with a statistics background to pursue careers in government and health policy. Drawing on the speaker's experience working in a local government setting, for a federal government contractor, and in an academic health services research setting, this talk gives an overview of how statisticians can successfully collaborate with interdisciplinary teams to contribute to improvements in health programs and policy. This talk will also discuss factors to consider when deciding whether to pursue a PhD for students interested in health policy and public sector careers.

Amber Robbins
— State of Montana
Statistics in the Wild

Statistics rarely arrive as neatly defined textbook problems. This presentation uses three real-world examples from the Montana Legislative Audit Division (gravel pits, affordable housing, and the state Motor Pool) to show how quantitative methods can help answer practical questions facing state government. Through examples involving causal inference, spatial analysis, and optimization, the presentation explores how statisticians can translate messy policy questions into defensible evidence, turning analysis into something policymakers can actually use.

Contributed Talks

Kunal Das
— School of Computing, Montana State University
State space models for multivariate time series data with nonmonotone MNAR missingness

State-space models are widely used for analyzing multivariate time series with missing observations. In many longitudinal studies, the pattern of missingness is nonmonotone, and the probability of missingness depends directly on unobserved signal values, leading to nonmonotone Missing Not At Random (MNAR) mechanisms that induce systematic bias under the widely popular MAR-based state-space inferences developed in the literature so far. We propose linear-Gaussian state-space models with explicit MNAR modeling for such data, where the missingness process is governed by a logistic regression depending on latent states, current and lagged emissions, and historical missingness. By leveraging Polya–Gamma augmentation, the MNAR mechanism is rendered conditionally conjugate. This enables closed-form posterior updates via Gibbs sampling and variational inference via Kalman filtering and Rauch–Tung–Striebel smoothing within a coordinate-ascent algorithm. Through simulation studies and an application on oceanographic dataset, we demonstrate the advantages of our method over existing state-of-the-art approaches.

Will Hammond
— Montana State University
Reconstructing Environmental Systems from Irregular Observations with CRU–SHRED

Recurrent neural networks have shown promising performance in the modelling of real-world spatiotemporal data. Among the many deep learning approaches to this problem, SHallow REcurrent Decoder (SHRED) networks use spatially sparse sensor data to reconstruct full system states across time, motivated by Takens’ embedding theorem. Prior SHRED networks have used long-short-term-memory (LSTM) and gated recurrent units (GRU), which are best suited to complete, synchronously observed samples on a regular time grid. Environmental monitoring data, however, are frequently incomplete and irregularly sampled. In this work, we explore the use of continuous recurrent units (CRU) in a SHRED network. CRUs use Kalman-style predict and update steps to assimilate observations, allowing the model to account explicitly for variable time intervals and missing measurements. We evaluate the resulting CRU-SHRED architecture on global sea surface temperature data and compare its reconstruction with conventional LSTM- and GRU-based SHRED models under regular, irregular, and missing-data scenarios.

Kathi Irvine
— USGS
Between Evidence and Aspiration: Distinguishing Supportable Conclusions from Desired Ones

The validity of statistical inferences drawn from observational field studies, experimental designs, or probability sampling depends upon the decisions made prior, during, and after data collection. To support defensible scientific conclusions, analysts must distinguish between what the data can reliably tell us from what we hope they might reveal. We present a generalizable framework for evaluating datasets, which emphasizes four foundational design components: Spatial, Temporal, Response, and Inference Design (aka “STRIDe”). By applying an iterative, five step workflow grounded in clearly articulated objectives, STRIDe helps users diagnose misalignment between desired statistical inferences and the design architecture underlying any dataset. The framework uses structured critical thinking—supported by tools such as Ishikawa (fishbone) diagrams and targeted empirical or simulation based follow up explorations—to identify factors that may undermine defensible inference. Although applicable to any ecological dataset, we illustrate the approach in the context of long term monitoring, where evolving objectives, data integration challenges, and shifting analytical methods frequently introduce design–inference disconnects. Our workflow anchored by an easy to recall acronym, STRIDe, provides a transferable foundation for exploring the alignment between data structures and statistical inference goals—closing the gap between evidence and aspiration.

Lakviru Korathotage
— Montana State University
Addressing the Generalized Multivariate Behrens-Fisher Problem in High-Dimensional Data

This research addresses a critical limitation in the standard Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA), specifically its sensitivity to unequal group variances (heteroscedasticity). In high-dimensional distance- based situations, test statistics such as the standard pseudo-F (F1) can produce misleading results, with inflated Type I error rates. This is a challenge known as the Generalized Multivariate Behrens-Fisher problem. This study investigates the effectiveness of the recently proposed statistic, F2, as a solution to this problem in distance-based hypothesis testing. We utilized a simulation-based approach to compare the performance of the statistics F1 and F2. Synthetic high-dimensional datasets were generated with varying degrees of heteroscedasticity. Our findings demonstrate that F1 is consistently misled by heteroscedasticity, yielding smaller p-values (p < 0.05) even when group locations are identical. Conversely, F2 maintained nominal Type I error rates reasonably well by correctly partitioning location differences from dispersion effects. We developed the F2Adonis2 R package, providing a user-friendly tool for researchers to evaluate shifts in group centers in the presence of unequal noise levels. To demonstrate the use of F2 and our R package, the results are applied to real-world biological datasets.

Sarah Mensah
— Montana State University
Functional ANOVA Under Heteroskedasticity: A Framework for Within-Time Testing

Existing global tests for functional ANOVA perform well under homoskedasticity but many can be problematic when variability differs across groups. This issue arises in our motivating functional dataset, where heat flow curves from a biomedical experiment show differences in variance across treatment groups as well as possible differences in the means of the groups. The presence of heteroskedasticity can lead to inflated Type I error rates and reduced power to detect differences in group mean functions. To address this, simulation studies are conducted to assess the performance of existing global tests for functional ANOVA under conditions such as non-constant variance, unbalanced designs, and small sample sizes. We explore a combination of the best-performing global tests with a two-level within-time testing framework to identify specific times or regions where group mean curves differ and to conduct pairwise comparisons within those identified regions in the presence of heteroskedasticity.

Nicole Kamarat
— Montana State University
Permutation Exploration for Improvement of Scalable Spatial Stream Network Models and Knowledge of Freshwater Habitats

Protecting freshwater habitats is vital for supporting diverse fish, wildlife, and surrounding human communities. To accurately monitor these ecosystems, Spatial Stream Network (SSN) models were developed to account for the complex topology of river networks. Scalable Spatial Stream Network (S3N) models improve computational efficiency by utilizing a Nearest Neighbor Gaussian Process (NNGP) to optimize storage requirements for estimation and prediction. S3N and NNGP performance depend on observation point order, and Guinness (2018) demonstrated the choice of permutation can enhance model fit on rectangular planes. This study extends these findings to river networks by evaluating four distinct point-ordering permutations to determine their influence on S3N model goodness of fit and computational efficiency. Using simulated data, we investigate breadth-first or depth-first searches, originating from the most upstream or most downstream observation point. As this research is ongoing, preliminary findings on the impact of systematic versus random ordering will be presented.

Bernard Sefah
— Montana State University
Modeling Cross-Disease Vulnerability in Africa Using Environmental and Social Determinants to Predict Malaria Risk and Susceptibility to Dengue, Chikungunya, and Measles

Vector-borne and vaccine-preventable diseases such as malaria, dengue, chikungunya, and measles remain major public health challenges and a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in Africa, accounting for a significant proportion of the region’s disease burden. Factors including fragile health systems, rapid urbanization, and climate variability create an environment conducive to disease outbreaks and hinder socio-economic development. Despite the interconnected nature of these diseases, cross-disease vulnerability modeling using integrated environmental and social determinants remains limited in the African context.
